# How to Fix a Leaky Faucet

The rhythmic drip-drip-drip of a leaky faucet isn't just annoying — it's wasting water and money. A single dripping faucet can waste up to **3,000 gallons of water per year**. That's roughly $100 in wasted water costs annually.

The good news? Many common faucet leaks can be repaired yourself with simple tools and inexpensive replacement parts. Here's how.

> ⚠️ **Safety first:** Always wear **eye protection** when working under sinks — loosening fittings can send debris, water, or corrosion into your eyes. In older homes, faucets and pipes may contain **lead** or lead solder, so wash your hands after handling and avoid getting water in your mouth. Some jurisdictions require a **permit** or a licensed plumber for certain plumbing work, especially where building codes apply — check your local rules before starting. If you're unsure, or if the work involves gas or involves modifying supply lines, **consult a licensed plumber**.

## Identify Your Faucet Type

Before fixing anything, determine the type of faucet you have. This determines which part needs replacing.

| Faucet Type | How to Identify | Common Failure |
|-------------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Compression** | Two separate handles (hot and cold) | Worn rubber washer |
| **Ball** | Single lever handle | Worn O-rings or springs |
| **Cartridge** | Single handle (comes in many styles) | Worn cartridge or O-ring |
| **Ceramic disk** | Single lever, very smooth action | Dirty or cracked ceramic disks |

## Step 1: Prep — Gather Tools & Turn Off Water

**You'll need:**
- Adjustable wrench
- Flathead and Phillips screwdrivers
- Allen wrench (hex key) set
- Replacement parts (washer, O-rings, or cartridge)
- Plumber's tape
- A cloth or paper towels

**Turn off the water:**
1. Look **under the sink** for shutoff valves on the hot and cold supply lines.
2. **Turn the valves clockwise** to close them.
3. If there are no shutoff valves, **turn off the main water valve** and open a lower sink faucet to drain residual water.

## Step 2: Fix a Compression Faucet (Two Handles)

**Most common type for older homes.**

1. **Remove the handle cap** (small cover on top) to expose the screw beneath.
2. **Remove the screw** and pull off the handle.
3. **Remove the packing nut** on the valve stem (should be a 6-sided nut).
4. **Pull out the stem** assembly.
5. **Look at the bottom** — you'll see the rubber washer held by a small brass screw.
6. **Remove the screw**, the old washer, and install the new one.
7. **Don't forget** to also check the O-ring around the stem (replace if cracked or worn).
8. **Reassemble** everything in reverse order.
9. **Turn the water back on** and test.

> **Order to check:** If it leaks around the handle (not the spout), the packing nut or O-ring needs replacing. If it drips from the spout, the washer is worn.

## Step 3: Fix a Cartridge Faucet (Single Handle)

**Most common type in modern homes.**

1. **Remove the handle** — usually held by a small set screw (Allen wrench) or a screw under a cap.
2. **Remove the metal retaining** nut or clip (may need pliers or an Allen wrench).
3. **Pull out the cartridge** using needle-nose pliers or a cartridge puller.
4. **Take the old cartridge** to a hardware store to get an exact match (they come in many sizes).
5. **Insert the new cartridge** and secure it.
6. **Reassemble** and test.

> **Cost:** Cartridges cost $5-30, which is far cheaper than a plumber's visit.

## Step 4: Fix a Ball Faucet

**Older single-handle type — identifiable by a rounded, rotating ball mechanism.**

1. **Remove the handle** and the three screws on the ring around the ball.
2. **Pull the cap, ball, and seals** out of the body.
3. **Inspect the O-rings, springs, and seals** — replace any that look worn.
4. **Replace the ball** if it's scratched or pitted.
5. **Reassemble** — there's a specific way the ball aligns, so note the position before removal.

## Step 5: Fix a Ceramic Disk Faucet

**The most modern type — very reliable, rarely leaks.**

1. **Remove the handle.**
2. **Remove the cartridge** (the ceramic disk assembly).
3. **Check the ceramic disks** for cracks or damage.
4. **Replace the cartridge** if damaged.
5. Often, simply **cleaning the disks** with a soft cloth fixes the problem — mineral buildup can prevent a proper seal.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- ✗ **Forgetting to turn off the water** — always do this first.
- ✗ **Overtightening** washers and nuts — hand-tight plus a quarter turn is usually enough.
- ✗ **Using the wrong replacement part** — take the old part to the hardware store with you.
- ✗ **Not wrapping new threads** with plumber's tape — prevents leaks at the connections.

## When to Call a Plumber

While most faucet leaks are DIY-friendly, call a professional if:

- You have a **newer, high-tech faucet** (touchless, sensor, or multi-function) — these often need specialized service.
- The faucet body is **cracked or corroded**.
- There's **water damage** under the sink.
- You don't have the **correct tools** or confidence.
- The leak persists **after replacing the washer/cartridge** — the valve seat may need resurfacing.

*Costs and times vary widely depending on your location, faucet type, and whether additional parts or repairs are needed.*

## The Bottom Line

Many faucet leaks can be repaired at home by identifying the faucet type, turning off the water, and replacing the worn washer, O-ring, or cartridge. Parts are typically inexpensive, and the process is often straightforward for basic faucet types.

Even if you've never fixed a faucet before, this is generally one of the more beginner-friendly DIY repairs. The satisfaction of stopping that drip is well worth it.
